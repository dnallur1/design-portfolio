03 / GAME PROPOSAL · WEEK 1 · THEME: HALLUCINATION

Out of Reflection

The clearer it becomes, the less you trust it.

01

Introduce
the game.

Game description

Out of Reflection is a short observation puzzle game exploring the theme of hallucination. The player compares a room with its blurred reflection and clicks objects that do not match. Each correct discovery brings the mirror into focus while changing the remaining mismatches.

Across three continuous stages in the same room, the player notices obvious differences, discovers finer details, and eventually finds that selecting objects in the mirror changes the room itself.

Player experience

The player begins with curiosity and a simple goal: make the reflection clear. As familiar details shift, they question their memory and which version of the room they can trust. The final reflection is sharp, but the room is no longer the one they started in.

Present your case study.

WE BECOME WHAT WE BEHOLD

We Become What We Behold is a short game by Nicky Case in which players photograph interactions between circle- and square-shaped characters. The pictures appear on a central news screen, influencing the crowd’s behavior. Through this cycle, the game explores how selective media coverage and audience attention can amplify conflict, fear, and division.

Created by Nicky Case · Play the original game ↗

Three meaningful moments.

MOMENT 01

Discovering what counts as “interesting”

What I saw and did

I began playing without knowing what to expect from the camera. This photograph showed an empty part of the scene, and the game responded with “WOWWEE, IT’S NOTHING.”

What seemed possible / what changed

At first, I was exploring what I could photograph. The dismissive caption suggested that capturing any part of the scene would not necessarily produce a meaningful response. It encouraged me to look for noticeable actions or unusual subjects.

What I understood afterward

I began to understand that the game judged my photographs through its captions. I could choose where to point the camera, but its feedback shaped what I looked for next.

MOMENT 02

Trying peace after seeing anger spread

What I saw and did

After photographing conflict and noticing more shapes turning red and appearing angry, I searched for peaceful moments. This image shows a smiling character wearing a heart, with the caption “peace is boring. violence goes viral.”

What seemed possible / what changed

I thought photographing something peaceful might change the crowd’s behavior. However, I noticed no improvement from my attempts. After continuing to search for peaceful subjects, I returned to photographing conflict because those pictures had produced visible changes earlier.

What I understood afterward

I interpreted the response as pressure to prioritize conflict. The game’s feedback influenced my behavior: even when I wanted to highlight peace, conflict seemed to be the way to make something happen.

MOMENT 03

Facing the consequences at the end

What I saw and did

As I continued photographing violence, I observed it growing. The ending then showed framed portraits and candles around a dark television, while a circle and square stood together beneath a red heart.

What seemed possible / what changed

The scene shifted from escalating violence to a still, dark setting that looked like a memorial. The heart brought my attention back to the peaceful connection I had tried to photograph earlier.

What I understood afterward

I interpreted the ending as mourning the consequences of the violence. Looking back, my camera seemed to have participated in the cycle: I photographed conflict, it received attention, and the violence grew. The ending made the earlier dismissal of peaceful moments feel more significant.

INTENT, OBSERVATION & INTERPRETATION

Three kinds of evidence.

01

Creator statement

In We Become What We Behold, Nicky Case intended to explore how media and audience behavior reinforce cycles of conflict, distrust, and social polarization. In the creator’s post-mortem, Case explains that the game emerged from frustration with sensationalist news and the feedback loop between what media publishes and what audiences choose to consume.

Case’s central design intention was to make this feedback loop visible through both visual composition and gameplay. Players repeatedly photograph and publish moments from the lives of Circles and Squares, experiencing how the selection of conflict can encourage further hostility. Following playtesting, Case also introduced interconnected character stories to create a stronger emotional connection. The post-credits scene, showing a Circle and a Square remaining united, introduces a possibility of resilience and reconciliation.

The project therefore uses interactive storytelling to encourage reflection on media responsibility, audience participation, and the social consequences of the stories people choose to amplify.

02

My observation

When I started playing, I did not know what to expect. I took photographs and saw captions appear. After photographing an angry circle near a square, I received the headline “CIRCLES HATE SQUARES.” As I continued photographing conflict, I noticed more shapes turning red and appearing angry.

I then searched for peaceful moments. Photographs of peaceful subjects received captions such as “peace is boring. violence goes viral” and “GIVE THE AUDIENCE WHAT THEY WANT.” I noticed no improvement, so I returned to photographing conflict. Later, a character appeared with a gun. As I continued capturing the violence, I observed it growing, while “BE SCARED. BE ANGRY.” appeared repeatedly. The ending showed portraits and candles around a dark television, with a circle and square together beneath a heart.

03

My interpretation

I interpreted the camera as a tool that influenced the community. The headline about circles turned one character’s anger into a claim about a whole group, suggesting how selective images and broad captions can encourage distrust.

The feedback also shaped my behavior. I wanted to see whether peaceful photographs could improve the situation, but after noticing no change, I returned to conflict because it had produced visible responses. This made me feel involved in the cycle: I sought conflict, photographed it, and watched hostility grow. That experience connects with Case’s stated interest in both media and audience participation.

I read the final scene as a memorial, while the heart suggested a remaining possibility of connection. However, that hopeful image did not give me a clear way to reverse the violence during play. For me, the ending suggested hope while the gameplay emphasized how difficult the cycle was to interrupt.

REVIEW WHAT I LEARNED

How the discussion changed my thinking.

01

What did AI help you notice?

AI helped me consider how the game’s feedback shaped my choices. I had noticed the crowd becoming angrier, but our discussion highlighted that I also changed my behavior: when peaceful photographs produced no noticeable improvement, I returned to conflict. This helped me connect the crowd’s reactions with my own participation in the cycle.

02

What did you challenge or correct?

I corrected the AI when it described the screenshots mainly as a sequence I witnessed. I explained that “the violence grew as I kept capturing it.” My experience over time added information that individual screenshots could not show, leading to a revised interpretation in which the camera seemed to contribute to escalation.

03

What is your own conclusion?

My conclusion is that the game explores both personal responsibility and the pressure created by a system’s feedback. I chose what to photograph, but the captions and visible responses encouraged certain choices. This matters because the game communicates its argument through actions and consequences that I experienced while playing.

04

How will you use this insight?

In my game proposal, I could make a repeated player action produce visible consequences in the game world. I would also decide deliberately how the game responds when players try an alternative approach. Playtesting would help me check whether players understand those consequences and whether the feedback encourages the behavior I intend.

04

Narrative and concept.

For Out of Reflection, the narrative can unfold through the room’s changes and the player’s discoveries.

Narrative premise

The player enters a quiet, familiar-looking room, but its blurred reflection contains objects that do not match. They begin finding these differences to bring the mirror into focus. Each discovery makes the reflection clearer while changing the remaining mismatches. As they continue, the player starts questioning what they remember—and whether the mirror is reflecting the room or reshaping it.

The player’s journey

  1. 01

    Arrive — Familiarity

    A room contains ordinary objects: a chair, a clock, books, and an empty vase. Its reflection is blurred. A short instruction invites them to find what does not match.

  2. 02

    Wonder — First discovery

    The player notices a flower in the reflected vase. Clicking it removes the flower and makes the mirror slightly clearer. Their action seems to restore order.

  3. 03

    Doubt — An observation disappears

    They remember seeing another mismatch: an open book reflected as closed. After selecting the flower, they look back—but both books are now open. A different mismatch has appeared elsewhere.

  4. 04

    Change — The mirror affects the room

    Later, another flower appears in the reflected vase. This time, selecting it makes a flower appear in the actual room. The player’s action has changed the space they were using as a reference.

  5. 05

    Leave — Clarity without certainty

    The final discovery clears the mirror completely. Room and reflection match, but familiar objects have changed. The player finishes the puzzle while wondering which version of the room was real.

EMOTIONAL MOVEMENT

Curiosity → confidence → doubt → surprise → uncertainty

Clicking a mismatch remains the main action throughout; its changing consequences tell the story.

05

Interaction and player journey.

Out of Reflection unfolds in one room across three continuous stages. The player clicks mismatches in the reflection. Each correct discovery earns progress, reduces the blur, and changes the remaining mismatches.

Six discoveries · Two per stage · One continuous room

01

Opening — Enter

What appears

A clear room beside its blurred reflection. A large flower appears only in the reflected vase. Instruction: “Click what doesn’t match.”

What the player does

Compares the two views and locates the flower.

Game response and continuation

The scene waits for their selection.

02

Stage 1 — Notice

What appears

Large, readable differences, such as an extra flower or a missing lamp.

What the player does

Clicks a mismatch in the reflection.

Game response and continuation

A circle and chime confirm success. The counter increases, the mirror sharpens, and the remaining mismatches change. After two discoveries, finer details become readable.

03

Stage 2 — Explore

What appears

Smaller differences in book covers, vase patterns, and object positions. A previously vanished mismatch returns in the same place.

What the player does

Looks again after each discovery, checking what is currently visible.

Game response and continuation

Correct selections clear more blur and replace the remaining mismatches. After four discoveries, the next stage begins without leaving the room.

04

Stage 3 — Reconsider

What appears

A flower appears again in the reflected vase while the real vase is empty.

What the player does

Clicks the reflected flower, expecting it to disappear.

Game response and continuation

The flower appears in the real vase instead. Both objects briefly glow to show the connection. The player continues searching as further selections change the room.

05

Ending — Leave

What appears

After the sixth discovery, the reflection is fully sharp. Room and reflection match, but the room differs from its opening appearance.

What the player does

Observes the final scene, then selects replay or exit.

Game response and continuation

Changes stop and the ambience settles, giving the player a moment to reflect.

06

Style and sound.

For Out of Reflection, I’d start with a simple illustrated room and quiet, slightly unfamiliar sounds. The objects should be easy to compare, while the atmosphere gradually creates doubt.

THE VISUAL LANGUAGE

Familiar room.
Unfamiliar reflection.

Flat illustration · clear outlines · a cooler mirror

  • Warm cream
  • Muted blue
  • Muted green
  • Charcoal

Visual direction

01 / Art style

Flat 2D illustration with clean outlines and minimal texture.

Keeps small differences readable.

02 / Composition

Two equally sized views: the room and its mirrored version, clearly separated by a mirror frame.

Helps players compare objects and understand where to click.

03 / Colour

Warm cream walls, muted blue and green objects, dark charcoal outlines. Give the reflection a slightly cooler tint.

Makes the room feel familiar while giving the reflection a subtle sense of distance.

04 / Blur

Blur only the reflection, reducing it after each correct discovery. Keep large shapes recognisable.

Shows progress and gradually reveals finer details.

05 / Typography

A plain, readable sans-serif for instructions and the progress counter.

Keeps attention on observation.

06 / Feedback

A brief circle around a correct selection, followed by a gentle ripple across the mirror.

Confirms success and signals that the remaining differences have changed.

Keep the room’s layout and viewpoint consistent across all three stages. Let uncertainty come from the objects.

SOUND, SILENCE & RHYTHM

From calm to uncertainty.

  1. Searching

    Quiet room tone with faint clock ticking.

    Establishes an ordinary, calm space.

  2. Correct selection

    A short, soft glass chime.

    Gives reliable confirmation of success.

  3. Differences change

    A gentle wavering echo after the chime.

    Signals that the player should inspect the scene again.

  4. Incorrect selection

    A soft, dull tap paired with a small visual ripple.

    Encourages another attempt without interrupting concentration.

  5. Later stages

    The background ticking occasionally skips or repeats.

    Makes the familiar atmosphere feel less dependable.

  6. Ending

    The wavering ambience fades, followed by a final chime.

    Creates a clear stopping point while leaving the visual uncertainty unresolved.

The success chime stays consistent, even as the ambience becomes strange. Every necessary sound cue will have visible feedback, so the game remains playable even when muted.

“Visually clear, quietly unsettling, and increasingly uncertain.”

Build a playable prototype

  1. 01

    Create the scene

    Produce initial artwork for one room and its reflection, with recognisable objects and readable differences.

  2. 02

    Build the main interaction

    Let players click mismatches, receive feedback, and earn permanent progress.

  3. 03

    Make the reflection change

    Reduce blur after correct selections and replace the remaining mismatches. Allow some differences to return later.

  4. 04

    Develop the three-stage journey

    Move from obvious mismatches to finer details, then introduce selections that change the real room.

  5. 05

    Add sound and feedback

    Include discovery sounds, quiet ambience, and visual cues that signal changes.

  6. 06

    Connect the opening and ending

    Add instructions, a completion state, and replay. Check that the full experience works before testing.

DeliverableA prototype playable from beginning to end, ready for Week 3 testing.

WEEK 3

Observe, test, and refine

  1. 01

    Observe first attempts

    Check whether players understand what to compare and where to click.

  2. 02

    Test visibility and difficulty

    Identify whether blur hides answers or whether differences become too easy or difficult.

  3. 03

    Check the changing rules

    Observe whether players notice shifting mismatches and understand that progress remains earned.

  4. 04

    Evaluate the experience

    Ask whether returning objects and room changes create the intended uncertainty.

  5. 05

    Refine from feedback

    Adjust artwork, blur, pacing, sound, and instructions. Simplify or change features where testing shows a need.

  6. 06

    Retest the revised prototype

    Verify that the changes improve understanding and that the complete journey still works.
